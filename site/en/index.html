DNS in two minutes

DNS is the internet's address book

On the internet, every site lives at an address made of numbers, such as 185.15.58.224. Handy for machines, impossible for us to remember.

DNS (the Domain Name System) makes the link: you type a name, it finds the number. Just like a phone book.

It happens hundreds of times a day without you noticing: every site, every app, every picture, every video and every update starts with a question to the DNS.

en.wikipedia.org
Your computer

What is the address of en.wikipedia.org?

The address book (the DNS)

It's 185.15.58.224.

The page shows up in the blink of an eye.

But who keeps the address book?

Unless you chose otherwise, it is usually your internet provider, through your router. Sometimes Google (8.8.8.8) or Cloudflare (1.1.1.1), if someone changed the setting.

That address book receives every question you ask, one after the other, with the time. And since it is the one answering, it can also decide that a site does not exist, or send you somewhere else.

With OffLok, that address book is your own computer.

Your privacy

No address book receives the list of your visits any more. Each server only learns what it needs to know, and OffLok keeps no record of what you look up.

Your freedom

Answers come from the source, not from a middleman choosing for you what exists. Want to block something? You decide what.

An internet that stays up

Your address book depends on no company: if your provider's DNS goes down, yours keeps going. And what it has already found stays in memory, even when a server stops answering.

Answers that are checked

When a name is signed (DNSSEC, a kind of digital seal), OffLok checks the seal on your computer and rejects a forged answer.

Fewer trackers, if you want

Blocking trackers and malicious sites is included, off at first: one switch per category, your own lists, a 10-minute pause, and “Trust this site” for one you miss.

Free, open, no account

The code is public (MIT licence): anyone can read and check it. No account, no ads, no subscription, no statistics sent.

What OffLok does not do

We'd rather tell you straight.

  • It does not encrypt your questions.Like all classic DNS, they travel in the clear: your provider or the Wi-Fi can still read them as they pass. They just no longer receive them to answer them.
  • It is not a VPN.Sites see your address, and your provider sees which servers you connect to.
  • The first visit is a little slower.A fraction of a second, the time to walk down the tree. After that, the answer is already on your computer.
  • It does not make you anonymous.And it changes nothing about the law: what is forbidden stays forbidden.

Check what you downloaded (optional, for the curious)

Every release comes with a SHA256SUMS file, signed with OffLok's release key (offlok-release-45e724ba.pub.pem, Ed25519). Download SHA256SUMS and SHA256SUMS.sig next to your files, then, with OpenSSL 3:

openssl pkeyutl -verify -pubin -inkey offlok-release-45e724ba.pub.pem -rawin -in SHA256SUMS -sigfile SHA256SUMS.sig
sha256sum --ignore-missing -c SHA256SUMS

Automatic updates do this check by themselves: an update installs only if its signature is right.

A bit of history

A short history of the internet's address book

DNS is more than forty years old. Its history shows why whoever keeps the address book matters so much.

  1. 1970s

    One file for the whole network

    In the days of ARPANET, the internet's ancestor, the list of names and addresses fits in a single file, HOSTS.TXT, kept up to date at SRI in California and copied by every computer. The bigger the network grows, the less that works.

  2. 1983

    DNS is born, shaped like a tree

    Paul Mockapetris, at the University of Southern California's Information Sciences Institute, invents DNS (RFC 882 and 883, November 1983): an address book spread out as a tree. At the top the root, then .com, .org, .fr…, each branch kept by someone else. That is the tree OffLok's logo draws.

  3. 1987

    Today's rules

    RFC 1034 and 1035 set DNS down as we still use it, nearly forty years later.

  4. 2008

    The Kaminsky flaw, and Unbound

    Researcher Dan Kaminsky finds that fake answers can be slipped into address books, sending their users to fake sites. On 8 July 2008, vendors around the world release a fix together. The same year, the Dutch foundation NLnet Labs releases Unbound, the free address book OffLok runs on your computer.

  5. 2009

    The giants step in

    Google launches its public address book, 8.8.8.8, on 3 December 2009; Cloudflare follows with 1.1.1.1 on 1 April 2018. Fast and handy, but millions of people's questions end up with a handful of companies.

  6. 2010

    A seal on the root

    On 15 July 2010, the root is signed (DNSSEC): an answer can now be checked, like a wax seal, all the way to the top of the tree. OffLok does that check itself.

  7. 2016

    Google “blocked for terrorism”… by mistake

    On 17 October 2016, for about an hour, customers of the French provider Orange trying to open google.fr, Wikipedia or OVH land on the French Interior Ministry's page for terrorist sites. A human error in Orange's address book, which shows that whoever keeps the address book decides what you see.

  8. 2016

    Only say what's needed

    Stéphane Bortzmeyer, of Afnic (which runs .fr), publishes query “minimisation” (RFC 7816, March 2016): ask each server only what it needs to know. OffLok uses it: the root only sees “org”, not the whole site.

  9. 2024

    Public address books filtered in turn

    At Canal+'s request, French courts order Google, Cloudflare and Cisco to block, in their public address books, sites streaming sport illegally; Cisco chooses to shut down its OpenDNS service in France. Whatever goes through an address book can be filtered by it.

  10. 2026

    A new key for the root, and OffLok

    On 11 October 2026, the root changes its signing key, for the second time in its history. On 30 October, OffLok 0.5.0 comes out as free software: the DNS tree, walked by your own computer, from a root copied in more than 2,000 places around the world.

Questions

You may be wondering…

Could it break my internet?

No. Every change is tested; at the slightest failure, OffLok puts your original settings back by itself and tries again a minute later. Turning it off or uninstalling it leaves your computer exactly as before.

Does it slow down my connection?

The first visit to a site takes a fraction of a second longer, the time to walk down the tree from the root. After that, the answer is kept on your computer: it comes at once, without even going over the network.

Can my internet provider still see anything?

It no longer receives your questions to answer them, but they travel in the clear over its network: it could read them as they pass, just as it sees the addresses of the servers you connect to. OffLok removes the middleman, not the pipe.

To hide the pipe too, you need a VPN or Tor, which are other tools.

What about a hotel's or a station's Wi-Fi?

These networks often block direct access to the root, or ask you to sign in on a page. OffLok then goes through the network's DNS until it can come back (the icon with an orange dot).

Would you rather never go through it? Turn on strict mode: “Sign in to this network” opens the door for 5 minutes, the time of the sign-in page. You can also set an encrypted fallback resolver (DNS over TLS), used before the network's DNS.

Does it block sites?

If you want. Blocking is included but off at first, with three switches: trackers, malicious sites (phishing, scams) and your own lists. The lists come from their authors (HaGeZi, URLhaus and others), downloaded once a day by your computer.

A site stopped working? “Check” tells you whether it is blocked and by which list, and “Trust this site” unblocks it for good.

It's free? What's the catch?

No catch: OffLok is free software (MIT licence). Its code is public; no account, no ads, no subscription, and it sends no statistics.

If you want to support its development, you can donate on Liberapay, a non-profit donation platform based in France.

What it contacts, besides DNS servers: the blocking lists you switch on, and GitHub once a day to see whether there is a new version. That last check can be turned off with a switch.

Why do Windows and macOS warn me during installation?

Windows is wary of programs without a signature it recognises. A free signature for open-source software (SignPath Foundation) is being prepared for OffLok. Meanwhile, the installer is built in public by GitHub from the published code, and you can check it was not altered (see “Check what you downloaded”).

On a Mac, same reason: OffLok is not signed by Apple yet. The steps are in the Mac card above.

Is it legal?

Yes. Running your own DNS address book is perfectly normal: companies, universities and internet providers do exactly the same. OffLok does not make you anonymous and changes nothing about the law: what is forbidden stays forbidden.

How do I uninstall it?

On Windows: Settings › Apps › Installed apps, then OffLok › Uninstall. On Linux: sudo apt remove offlok offlok-desktop (or sudo dnf remove on Fedora). On a Mac: open Terminal, type sudo /usr/local/libexec/offlok/uninstall.sh, then your password.

Either way, every connection of your computer gets its original DNS back.

Who makes OffLok?

OffLok is developed by LokLakh and published as free software: anyone can read its code, check it and suggest improvements on GitHub. It relies on Unbound, the free address book from the NLnet Labs foundation.
